AN IMMERSIVE CASE STUDY EXERCISE

The Back Row: Marco

You are a university instructor, not a therapist. Marco is in your course. His reputation arrived before he did: difficult, argumentative, disrespectful. He is also sharp, funny, and, according to his classmates, brave. Over one semester, you will be tested on one question above all: are you the kind of authority he has learned to look for, or the kind he has learned to detect?

Two gauges track him: Guard, how hard he is bracing for unfair or false authority, and Trust, how safe he is starting to feel with you. Rank, scripts, and presumptions raise his guard fast, even when they sound kind. Push him over the edge and he takes the floor. There is no perfect script.

Your semester, moment by moment

The numbers appear here for the first time. Read what Marco was detecting in each choice: with him, what sounded kind and what sounded fair were often different things.

Reading your trace

  • Where did his guard climb fastest, and what in your behavior did he read as false, cruel, or intoxicated by authority?
  • Which of your choices sounded warm but were scripted? How would you have known he would hear them that way?
  • Where did you act on a story you already believed about him, and what would a different story have changed?

Take it to your group

Before anything else: choose one observation about Marco that surprised you or that you couldn't fully explain. You do not need written answers. The discussion is not about generating answers, but about deepening, complicating, and turning them. The first (and usually most obvious) answer is your beginning, not your destination.

  • He was called difficult, argumentative, and rebellious from very early on. If you read the case again, what was he actually responding to? Before judging the behavior, reconstruct its logic: what did Marco understand about his environment that the adults around him seemed to miss?
  • A classmate arrives with a black eye from his father and blames Marco, and no adult meaningfully questions it. What might that have done to Marco's relationship with authority, with fairness, and with the idea that truth matters? What does it tell us about how schools, or other institutions, use the stories they already believe?
  • His protests (over food, arbitrary rules, a humiliated classmate) were appreciated by peers and found disruptive by teachers. Who was right? Is there a version of this behavior that a different teacher might have seen as leadership, moral sensitivity, or civic courage? What stopped that from happening?
  • His behavior looks very different depending on the teacher: engaged with safe adults, weaponized wit with those he has judged to be false authority. Is that inconsistency a problem to fix, or information? What does it say about the relationship between behavior and the environment that shaped it, and what does it demand of teachers?
  • Even at university, the old stance returns the moment a teacher seems performative, cruel, or drunk on authority, only now with wit instead of open rebellion. Is that growth, or the same wound in a smarter disguise? In your genuine opinion, was his read on those teachers usually wrong?
  • If you were his teacher (the good kind, whom he could trust), what would you need to understand to reach him? What would you not do, and why? And the harder version: what do teachers owe students who arrive already convinced that authority cannot be trusted?

When you have an initial answer, stay with it a little longer:

  • What in the story makes us think that?
  • What else could explain the same behavior?
  • What would we need to know to tell the difference?

Changing your mind, questioning an interpretation, and noticing missing information all count as contributions. You don't need to share personal experiences. Please discuss without AI.
